01 / The ask, in plain terms

One corner of one roof, this summer.

I’m not asking you to fund anything, sign a long lease, or take on a construction project. I’m asking for access to a section of usable roof for a growing season, on terms that work for the building.

Space — about 400 sq ft, in one corner
Roughly the footprint of a small bedroom. If your engineer or your roofer says less, it’s less.
Term — one growing season
This summer through October. Not a lease. Not a renewal you have to remember to cancel.
My hours — ~20 minutes most mornings, longer on harvest days
On a fixed schedule you set. Only I go up. No residents, no volunteers, no tours, no events.
What comes up — folding tables, shallow trays, a watering can, jugs of water
Everything is freestanding and hand-carried.
What I never touch — your membrane, your drains, your standpipe, your bulkhead, your parapet edge
Nothing is drilled, bolted, glued, or ballasted. Ever.

What I’m not asking for: money, construction, roof penetrations, permanent structures, changes to your building, or anything that can’t be undone in a day.

Note — Every line in this block is something I will put in a written agreement. If a line is wrong for your building, tell me on the call and it changes.

02 / What your building gets

I don’t expect free access to your roof.

Space in Manhattan is worth something, and I’d rather start this by saying that out loud. These aren’t a menu to choose from — tell me which direction makes sense for your building and we’ll work out the specifics.

A · Monthly rent
A flat monthly payment for the season, agreed before a single tray goes up. Predictable, and it doesn’t depend on whether I succeed. Think a few hundred dollars a month, not a few thousand — this is a folding table’s worth of space. I’ll bring a specific number to the call and I expect to be told if it’s wrong.
B · A share of what I sell
A percentage of revenue from everything grown on your roof, on a schedule we agree, with the numbers open to you. Costs the building nothing if I’m wrong. I’d only propose this to a building that finds the idea genuinely interesting, because it’s the version where you take a little of the risk with me.
C · A standing discount for your residents
Every household at the address buys at a permanent discount off what I charge everyone else, or gets a set amount free each harvest. No money moves between me and the building — the value goes straight to the people living in it. For a board that has to explain a decision to shareholders, this is usually the easiest one to explain.
If you have no preference
Modest monthly rent plus the resident discount. Certain income for the building, something residents can actually see, and simple enough to write down while we find out whether this works at all.

Whatever we agree on is in writing, it’s one season to start, and either of us can end it with 30 days’ notice. I’d rather have a short agreement you’re comfortable with than a long one you regret.

03 / The footprint

About 400 square feet in a corner. Not your roof.

Low folding tables on protective pads. Nothing bolted, drilled, or adhered to your membrane. Nothing stacked high enough to catch wind. I stay clear of the parapet edge, the bulkhead door, the standpipe, and anything your super needs to reach. If the building ever wants the space back, the whole setup comes off in a day — there’s nothing to demolish.

  1. Trays flat on low tables
  2. Pads or pavers under every leg
  3. Six-foot clear walking path maintained
  4. Set back from the parapet, never at the edge
  5. Water hand-carried, no plumbing
  6. Zero roof penetrations
  1. 10 × 20 inches. 1.4 square feet. Everything on this page scales from this one unit.
  2. Solid-bottom tray, bottom-watered. The water goes in the tray and stays in the tray. Nothing drains onto your roof.
  3. Growing medium comes up sealed and goes down sealed in the same bin. No open soil, no compost pile, ever.
  4. Folding table on rubber feet over pads. Nothing drilled, bolted, adhered, or ballasted into your membrane.
  5. No power, no plumbing, no pumps, no fans. Sun and a watering can.
  6. Cut at day 7 to 10, before it flowers or seeds. There’s nothing up here a pigeon or a rat wants.

04 / What this actually looks like

Manhattan has thousands of empty rooftops. I’d like to farm a corner of one.

Microgreens are grown in shallow trays, harvested in a week to ten days, and cut by hand. This is what a few hundred square feet of it looks like.

Most people hear “rooftop garden” and picture raised beds, soil, hoses, and a construction project. This isn’t that. It’s shallow trays on folding tables, in rows, in a corner of the roof. It’s quiet. It’s clean. It’s about a foot tall. And in ten days, it’s food.

05 / Why microgreens

Two numbers, and you can check both of them yourself.

7 to 10 days Seed to cut · observed

A full harvest cycle. Not a season — a week and a half, seed to cut. Most crops give you one or two harvests a summer. Microgreens give you a new one roughly every week on the same square footage.

$7 to $10 Per 3–4 oz container · shelf price, stores near W 85th

What a container costs at a grocery store on the Upper West Side right now. For a few ounces of greens with a short shelf life, cut days ago and trucked in from somewhere else.

Microgreens are the first true leaves of vegetables and herbs — sunflower, pea, radish, broccoli, cilantro — harvested seven to ten days after seeding. They’re harvested young, which is where the nutrient density and the flavour come from, and it’s also why they don’t need deep soil, a long season, or much space.

Almost all of the price on that shelf is distance. Growing on a roof on 85th Street and selling to people who live on 85th Street takes the trucks, the warehouse, and the shelf life out of it.

I’m not going to show you a revenue projection.

I haven’t grown a tray on your roof yet, and any number I put on this page would be a number I invented. What I’d rather tell you is where the constraint actually is. The demand is there. The price is there. The cycle is fast enough to make small spaces productive. The thing I don’t have is space. That’s why I’m writing to you.

06 / Your roof, answered honestly

The questions I’d ask if I were you.

You don’t know me. You’re being asked to let a stranger onto a roof you’re responsible for. Every answer is open on this page — nothing is hidden behind a click, so you can print it or forward it whole.

Nine questions, answered in full
Your concernHow I handle it
The roof membrane “Are you going to damage my roof membrane?”

This is the right question and the one I take most seriously — a membrane repair costs far more than anything I could pay you in rent. Nothing gets bolted, screwed, drilled, anchored, adhered, or ballasted. No penetrations of any kind. Everything is freestanding and movable, and trays sit on pads or a raised surface rather than directly on the membrane, so nothing sits wet against it and nothing scrapes it.

Before I bring anything up, I want to walk the roof with you or your super and photograph its condition, so there’s a shared record of what it looked like on day one. If your roofer has requirements or your warranty has terms, send them and I’ll design around them — or I’ll walk away from your roof rather than void a warranty.
Weight and load “How much does all this weigh?”

Microgreens are the lightest thing you can farm — shallow trays an inch or two deep, cut at 7 to 10 days, before they ever build the root mass or soil volume a vegetable planter needs. No raised beds, no barrels of standing water, nothing permanent. A watered tray is in the neighbourhood of a gallon of milk, and trays lie flat and spread out rather than stacking into towers.

Before anything goes up I’ll weigh a loaded tray on a scale and give you the total and the per-square-foot figure in writing. What I won’t do is tell you it’s fine for your specific roof — I don’t know your building’s structure and I’m not an engineer. If you or your board want a structural opinion first, that’s the right instinct, and I’d rather you have it than skip it.
Liability and insurance “Are you insured?”

Not yet, and I’m not going to tell you otherwise. I don’t have a policy because I don’t have a roof — nobody writes a policy for a site that doesn’t exist. Here’s the order, and it doesn’t move: you tell me you’re interested, I bind a general liability policy naming the building, the owner, and the managing agent as additional insureds, I send your management company the certificate, and only then does anything go up.

Tell me the limits you require of any vendor and I’ll meet them. You should ask me for the certificate rather than take my word for it. No certificate, no roof — I wouldn’t want it any other way.
Fire access and egress “What about fire access and egress?”

I stay clear of the bulkhead and the roof hatch, the standpipe, the ladder, any required path across the roof, and the parapet edge. Show me the clearances your building has to keep — or have your super walk them with me — and I’ll draw the footprint inside them and send you the drawing before I bring anything up.

If keeping those clearances means the usable corner is smaller than I hoped, the corner gets smaller. I’m not going to be the reason you get written up.
Water “What does this do to my water and my drains?”

Microgreens need far less water than a conventional garden — they’re harvested before they ever get big. Ideally there’s a spigot on the roof or on the top landing. If there isn’t, I carry water up in jugs; that’s normal for phase one and it isn’t a dealbreaker.

No hose is ever left running, nothing is watered without me there, and nothing drains toward roof drains or the parapet. If you’d rather not think about water usage at all, I’ll pay a flat monthly amount for it or cover a submeter — your call.
Access and keys “Who lets you in, and how often are you up there?”

Whatever your building’s policy is. If you don’t give out keys or fobs to vendors, I don’t need one — I’ll come during hours when the super can let me up, and I’ll work around the building’s schedule. If you do issue access to vendors, I’ll go through whatever your standard process is, sign in, and stay on the roof and the stairs and nowhere else.

Realistically this is about twenty minutes most mornings plus harvest days. Never at night. Only me — no residents, no volunteers, no tours.
Mess and noise “Is this going to be a mess, and will anyone hear it?”

There’s essentially no noise. No power tools, no machinery, no generator, nothing that runs unattended. Harvesting is a person with scissors and a container.

There’s no loose soil — everything is contained in trays. Spent trays and root mats go down with me in a sealed bag and out of the building; nothing accumulates on the roof, there’s no compost pile, and nothing goes in your bins. I clean up after every visit. If anything ever looks wrong to you up there, one text and I’ll be there that day to fix it.
Winter “What happens in the winter?”

Straight answer: this is a summer project first. The summer is a chance to prove the model outdoors before spending money on indoor space and grow lights, which is what winter production actually requires. I’m not asking your building to fund that, and I’m not going to promise you a year-round operation I haven’t earned yet.

If the summer works, I’d want to talk with you about what comes next — indoors, another season, or both. If it doesn’t work, I take everything down and the roof goes back exactly the way I found it.
Ending it “What if it doesn’t work — or you turn out to be a problem?”

Then it ends. Put a 30-day termination clause in whatever we sign, no cause required, and I’ll sign it without arguing. Everything I bring up is freestanding and comes down in a day, by hand. There’s nothing to demolish, nothing to unbolt, and nothing to remove but me.

That’s the entire reason phase one uses trays and folding tables instead of infrastructure.

08 / Phase one

What the first summer actually looks like.

Phase one is already underway — I’m growing, testing varieties, and dialling in the cycle. The thing I don’t have is the roof, which is what this page is for.

  1. Week 0

    We walk the roof together, with your super or your engineer. I take measurements and photos, then come back with a footprint drawn inside whatever limits they set. If it’s wrong for your building, we’ll both know in ten minutes and I’ll stop taking your time.

  2. Week 1

    Paperwork before product. Insurance certificate to your management company, a short written agreement with the 30-day termination clause in it, and an access schedule that you set.

  3. Week 2

    Setup. One day, everything hand-carried up. Folding tables, trays, pads, jugs of water.

  4. Day 7–10 after seeding

    First harvest. I bring some to your office.

  5. Through October

    Continuous cycles. Selling to your residents first, then to the block. I’ll tell you what’s working and what isn’t — including what isn’t.

  6. Winter

    If the summer proves out, it funds moving indoors with grow lights so production continues year-round. If it doesn’t, the roof goes back exactly how I found it, in a day.

The first real commitment you make is a phone call. Everything after that has an exit.

Danny Dalton. I live eight blocks from your building.

I’m one person, not a company with a marketing department, and this is phase one. There’s no farm to show you yet — that’s exactly why I’m writing to you. I’m not raising money, I don’t have a company behind me, and I’m not going to tell you I’ve done this before.
