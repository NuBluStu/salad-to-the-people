The math, all of it

Why microgreens, and why now.

Microgreens are the first true leaves of vegetables and herbs — sunflower, pea, radish, broccoli, cilantro — harvested seven to ten days after seeding. They're harvested young, which is where the nutrient density and the flavor come from, and it's also why they don't need deep soil, a long season, or much space.

Two numbers, and you can check both of them yourself.

7–10 days

That's a full cycle, seed to harvest. Not a season. Not a quarter. A tray that's seeded on a Monday is on somebody's plate the following week, and the same square foot of roof does it again immediately.

$7–10 per 3–4 oz

That's what a container of microgreens costs at the grocery store right now on the Upper West Side. Walk into any market on Broadway and look. That price exists because the greens were grown somewhere far away, packed, trucked in, and handled by several businesses before they got to the shelf — and because they're delicate enough that a lot of them don't survive the trip.

The whole idea is that almost all of that is distance. Growing on a roof on 85th Street and selling to people who live on 85th Street takes the trucks, the warehouse, and the shelf life out of the equation. That's what makes it possible to sell better greens for less money and still run a real business.

What I'm not going to show you.

I'm not going to put a revenue projection on this page. I haven't run a full season on a New York rooftop yet, and any five-year number I showed you would be a number I made up. The point of this summer is to find out what a roof in this city actually produces — real yields, real heat, real wind, real customers — before anybody makes claims about it.

What I can tell you is that the math is good enough to be worth finding out, that the cost to try it is low, and that I'm the one carrying that cost.

You pick how I pay you

What's in it for your building.

I'd rather structure this the way that's easiest for you than the way that's best for me. There are three ways I can pay for the space. Pick whichever one fits how your building actually works — or we combine them.

Rent

The simplest version. A flat monthly payment for the roof space, in a written agreement, month to month or seasonal. If your board or owner just wants a clean line item and no involvement, this is it.

A share of the profits

If you'd rather have upside than a fixed check, I'll pay the building a percentage of what the garden sells. It's less money on day one and more money if this works. I'll be straightforward about the numbers either way.

A standing discount for your residents

Instead of paying the building, I pay the people in it — residents buy at a standing discount off grocery-store pricing, delivered down the stairs instead of carried up from Broadway. For a co-op board, this is the version that's easiest to be proud of at a meeting.

Or some combination. Modest rent plus a resident discount is the structure I'd guess most buildings want. I'm genuinely flexible. This is the first roof, and getting it right matters more to me than squeezing it.

Whatever we land on goes in a written agreement before anything goes on the roof — including how either of us ends it, and how quickly I clear out.

One thing about rent, if you're a co-op.

I'm not your accountant and I'm not going to pretend to be. But I know a co-op has to watch what share of its gross income comes from people who aren't shareholders — the 80/20 test — and a rent check from me lands on the wrong side of that line. If that's a problem for your building, the resident-discount version costs the corporation nothing and doesn't touch that math at all. Ask your accountant. I'll structure it whichever way they say.

The part your roof cares about

The questions you're already asking.

You don't know me. You're being asked to let a stranger onto a roof you're responsible for. Here are honest answers, including where the honest answer is "we'd figure that out together."

Liability — who's responsible if something happens up there?

I am. I'll sign an agreement that says so, including indemnifying the building for anything arising from my work on the roof. I'd expect your management company or attorney to have standard language for exactly this — I'm not going to argue with it. If your building requires anyone on the roof to be escorted or to work only during certain hours, that's fine, and I'd rather you tell me your rules than have me propose my own.

The roof membrane. This is the one that costs real money.

Nothing gets drilled, screwed, anchored, or adhered. No penetrations of any kind. Everything is freestanding and sits on protective matting or pavers so the load spreads and nothing rests directly on the membrane. Nothing gets dragged across the roof.

Before we agree to anything, I'd like to walk the roof with you — and with your roofer or superintendent if you want them there. If your roof is under warranty and that warranty restricts who can be up there or what can sit on it, tell me and we work inside it or we don't do it. Whatever your roofer prefers is what I do, and I'll call him myself. A garden isn't worth a roof.

Weight and load.

This is much lighter than people picture. Microgreens grow in shallow trays — about an inch of growing medium, harvested in a week to ten days. There's no soil bed, no planter boxes, no standing water, no permanent structure. Trays sit on light racks that spread the weight over a wide footprint rather than concentrating it.

There is no weight printed on this page, because I haven't put a tray on a scale yet. Before you decide anything, I'll weigh a fully watered tray and a loaded rack section and give you the real numbers in writing, along with the exact square footage and layout I'm proposing.

And I'm not going to tell you your roof can take it, because I'm not an engineer. Give me a number from someone who is and I'll build to it, or I'll pay for the assessment myself. If your building already has load information for the roof, I'll size the garden to whatever it says. I'd rather start smaller than argue about it.

Water.

Microgreens need far less water than a conventional garden — they're harvested before they ever get big. Ideally there's a spigot on the roof or on the top landing. If there isn't, I carry water up in jugs; that's normal for phase one and it isn't a dealbreaker.

Trays are bottom-watered into catch trays, so what runs off goes into a tray and not onto your roof. No hose is ever left running, nothing is watered without me there, nothing is staged near a drain, and nothing drains toward roof drains or the parapet. I check and clear the nearest drain every visit and I'll send you the photo. If you'd rather not think about water usage at all, I'll pay a flat monthly amount for it or cover a submeter — your call.

Wind and weather.

Racks stay low and weighted, nothing taller than the parapet, nothing loose that can blow. Shade cloth comes down before weather, not after. Nothing up there can become a projectile in an August storm.

Fire, egress and clearances.

Bulkhead doors, vents, condensers, equipment clearances and every egress path stay clear. I'll walk the roof with you and your super before anything goes up and mark what's off limits, and I'll keep to whatever clearances the building or the FDNY require. If that means the garden gets smaller, the garden gets smaller.

Roof access and keys.

Whatever your building's policy is. If you don't give out keys or fobs to vendors, I don't need one — I'll come during hours when the super can let me up, and I'll work around the building's schedule. If you do issue access to vendors, I'll go through whatever your standard process is, sign in, and stay on the roof and the stairs and nowhere else. No guests, ever.

Realistically this is a few visits a week, mostly short ones, plus harvest days.

Insurance.

I'll carry a general liability policy and name the building — and the management company and owner, if you want them named — as additional insured. I'll send you the certificate before I bring a single tray up, not after. I'll sign your standard license and hold-harmless, and give you a W-9 whenever you want it.

I'm not going to pretend I already know what your building requires. Tell me the coverage and limits your management company asks of vendors and I'll match them. If those requirements make phase one impossible for a one-person operation, I'd rather find that out on a phone call in week one than waste your time.

Mess and noise.

There's essentially no noise. No power tools, no machinery, no generator, nothing that runs unattended. Harvesting is a person with scissors and a container.

There's no loose soil — everything is contained in trays. No compost pile, no fertilizer smell, no animals, no bees. Spent trays and root mats go down with me in a bag and out of the building; nothing accumulates on the roof and nothing goes in your compactor. I clean up after every visit. From the street, there is nothing to see. If anything ever looks wrong to you up there, one text and I'll be there that day to fix it.

What happens in the winter?

Straight answer: this is a summer project first. The summer is a chance to prove the model outdoors before spending money on indoor space and grow lights, which is what winter production actually requires. I'm not asking your building to fund that, and I'm not going to promise you a year-round operation I haven't earned yet.

So: if the summer works, I'd want to talk with you about what comes next — indoors, another season, or both. If it doesn't work, I take everything down and the roof goes back exactly the way I found it. Everything I bring up there, I can carry back down in a day. That's true on purpose.

And if you just want me gone.

Then I go. A one-page agreement, month to month, endable by you on short notice for any reason or none — no argument and no negotiation. I'll sign whatever your management company uses.

You are letting a stranger onto your roof. You should be able to reverse that decision easily. Everything comes off inside two days and the roof goes back exactly the way I found it; the whole setup fits in a freight elevator. I'd rather have an easy exit and a good relationship than a long contract and a manager who regrets it.
